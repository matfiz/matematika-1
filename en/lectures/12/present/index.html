Lecture 12

Vectors, length and projection

A displacement has components, but its length does not depend on the signs of those components. The dot product relates components to lengths and angles. We prove the inequalities that make these definitions consistent, then split a vector into a part along a given direction and an orthogonal remainder. The same split gives the closest point on a line through the origin.

Uses.

  • Summary 1: Ordered pairs have equal corresponding entries. Cartesian products collect such pairs.

  • Summary 2: Real arithmetic, nonnegative square roots and finite sums are available. Standing fact E gives cosine as a bijection from [0,π][0,\pi][0,π] onto [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1], with inverse arccos⁡\arccosarccos.

12.1 Coordinates and displacements

The Cartesian product R2=R×R\R^2=\R\times\RR2=R×R is the set of ordered pairs (x1,x2)(x_1,x_2)(x1​,x2​) of real numbers. The set R3=R×R×R\R^3=\R\times\R\times\RR3=R×R×R consists of ordered triples. More generally, for an integer n≥1n\geq1n≥1, the set Rn\R^nRn consists of ordered lists (x1,…,xn)(x_1,\ldots,x_n)(x1​,…,xn​) with every xj∈Rx_j\in\Rxj​∈R. Two lists are equal exactly when their corresponding entries are equal. An element of Rn\R^nRn is a vector. Its entries are its components. We write geometric vectors in bold, as u=(u1,…,un)\vv u=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)u=(u1​,…,un​).

In the coordinate plane and in three-dimensional space, we choose an origin and mutually perpendicular coordinate axes with the same unit of length. A point is specified by its coordinates. A directed segment from AAA to BBB has starting point AAA and end point BBB. Its displacement is the vector AB→=(b1−a1,…,bn−an).\overrightarrow{AB}=(b_1-a_1,\ldots,b_n-a_n).AB=(b1​−a1​,…,bn​−an​). Here AAA has coordinates (a1,…,an)(a_1,\ldots,a_n)(a1​,…,an​) and BBB has coordinates (b1,…,bn)(b_1,\ldots,b_n)(b1​,…,bn​), with n=2n=2n=2 or n=3n=3n=3. Segments with the same displacement represent the same vector even when their starting points differ. The position vector of a point is its displacement from the origin.

For example, take points with coordinates A=(1,2,−1),B=(4,−2,5).A=(1,2,-1),\qquad B=(4,-2,5).A=(1,2,−1),B=(4,−2,5). Their displacement is (4−1,−2−2,5−(−1))=(3,−4,6)(4-1,-2-2,5-(-1))=(3,-4,6)(4−1,−2−2,5−(−1))=(3,−4,6). Reversing the endpoints gives (−3,4,−6)(-3,4,-6)(−3,4,−6). A point and a displacement can have the same coordinate list, but they play different roles: a point specifies a location, while a displacement specifies a change.

12.2 Vector operations and standard coordinates

Let u\vv uu and v\vv vv belong to the same Rn\R^nRn, and let λ∈R\lambda\in\Rλ∈R. Their sum and the scalar multiple of u\vv uu are defined by u+v=(u1+v1,…,un+vn),λu=(λu1,…,λun).\vv u+\vv v=(u_1+v_1,\ldots,u_n+v_n),\qquad \lambda\vv u=(\lambda u_1,\ldots,\lambda u_n).u+v=(u1​+v1​,…,un​+vn​),λu=(λu1​,…,λun​). The identities λ(uj+vj)=λuj+λvj\lambda(u_j+v_j)=\lambda u_j+\lambda v_jλ(uj​+vj​)=λuj​+λvj​ and (λ+μ)uj=λuj+μuj(\lambda+\mu)u_j=\lambda u_j+\mu u_j(λ+μ)uj​=λuj​+μuj​ prove the two distributive laws. Similarly, λ(μuj)=(λμ)uj\lambda(\mu u_j)=(\lambda\mu)u_jλ(μuj​)=(λμ)uj​ proves compatibility of successive scalar multiplications. Equality of all components gives each vector identity.

Geometrically, adding displacements joins two successive directed segments. Indeed, for points AAA, BBB and CCC, component subtraction gives AB→+BC→=AC→.\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\overrightarrow{AC}.AB+BC=AC. A positive scalar stretches a segment without reversing it. A negative scalar reverses its direction as well as changing its length. Multiplication by zero gives the zero displacement.

A linear combination of vectors v1,…,vk\vv v_1,\ldots,\vv v_kv1​,…,vk​ is a sum c1v1+⋯+ckvkc_1\vv v_1+\cdots+c_k\vv v_kc1​v1​+⋯+ck​vk​ with coefficients cj∈Rc_j\in\Rcj​∈R. 12.3 The dot product and length

Definition 12.2 (Dot product and Euclidean norm). For u,v∈Rn\vv u,\vv v\in\R^nu,v∈Rn, their dot product is the real number u⋅v=∑j=1nujvj.\vv u\cdot\vv v=\sum_{j=1}^n u_jv_j.u⋅v=j=1∑n​uj​vj​. The distance between points with position vectors a\vv aa and b\vv bb is ∥b−a∥\norm{\vv b-\vv a}∥b−a∥.

The dot product is symmetric, since ujvj=vjuju_jv_j=v_ju_juj​vj​=vj​uj​. It is linear in each argument. For example, distributing each product in the finite sum gives (λu+μv)⋅w=λ(u⋅w)+μ(v⋅w).(\lambda\vv u+\mu\vv v)\cdot\vv w =\lambda(\vv u\cdot\vv w)+\mu(\vv v\cdot\vv w).(λu+μv)⋅w=λ(u⋅w)+μ(v⋅w). Consequently, ∥u∥≥0,∥u∥=0 ⟺ u=0,∥λu∥=∣λ∣∥u∥.\norm{\vv u}\geq0,\qquad \norm{\vv u}=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \vv u=\vv0,\qquad \norm{\lambda\vv u}=|\lambda|\norm{\vv u}.∥u∥≥0,∥u∥=0 ⟺ u=0,∥λu∥=∣λ∣∥u∥. 12.4 Length inequalities and angles

A finite family of vectors is linearly dependent if some real coefficients, not all zero, give a zero linear combination. It is linearly independent if a zero linear combination forces every coefficient to be zero. Thus independence is the negation of dependence. For a pair of vectors, dependence means that one is a scalar multiple of the other, including pairs containing zero. Indeed, if au+bv=0a\vv u+b\vv v=\vv0au+bv=0 and a≠0a\ne0a=0, then u=−(b/a)v\vv u=-(b/a)\vv vu=−(b/a)v. If a=0a=0a=0, then b≠0b\ne0b=0 and v=0\vv v=\vv0v=0. The converse follows by moving a scalar multiple to one side.

Theorem 12.4 (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality). For u,v∈Rn\vv u,\vv v\in\R^nu,v∈Rn, ∣u⋅v∣≤∥u∥∥v∥.|\vv u\cdot\vv v|\leq\norm{\vv u}\norm{\vv v}.∣u⋅v∣≤∥u∥∥v∥. Equality holds exactly when the two vectors are linearly dependent.

Proof. If v=0\vv v=\vv0v=0, both sides are zero and the pair is dependent. Suppose v≠0\vv v\ne\vv0v=0. Choose the scalar α=(u⋅v)/∥v∥2\alpha=(\vv u\cdot\vv v)/\norm{\vv v}^2α=(u⋅v)/∥v∥2. Bilinearity gives 0≤∥u−αv∥2=∥u∥2−2α(u⋅v)+α2∥v∥2=∥u∥2−(u⋅v)2∥v∥2.0\leq\norm{\vv u-\alpha\vv v}^2 =\norm{\vv u}^2-2\alpha(\vv u\cdot\vv v)+\alpha^2\norm{\vv v}^2 =\norm{\vv u}^2-\frac{(\vv u\cdot\vv v)^2}{\norm{\vv v}^2}.0≤∥u−αv∥2=∥u∥2−2α(u⋅v)+α2∥v∥2=∥u∥2−∥v∥2(u⋅v)2​. Multiplying by the positive squared norm proves the squared inequality. Taking nonnegative square roots gives the stated inequality. Equality holds exactly when u−αv=0\vv u-\alpha\vv v=\vv0u−αv=0. For nonzero v\vv vv this is exactly dependence of the pair. 

For example, take a=(−2,1)\vv a=(-2,1)a=(−2,1) and b=(4,−2)=−2a\vv b=(4,-2)=-2\vv ab=(4,−2)=−2a. Their dot product is −10-10−10, while their lengths are 5\sqrt55​ and 252\sqrt525​. Cauchy–Schwarz is an equality after taking the absolute value. A negative dot product does not prevent that equality.

For nonzero vectors, Cauchy–Schwarz places (u⋅v)/(∥u∥∥v∥)(\vv u\cdot\vv v)/(\norm{\vv u}\norm{\vv v})(u⋅v)/(∥u∥∥v∥) in [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1]. By standing fact E, precisely one θ∈[0,π]\theta\in[0,\pi]θ∈[0,π] has that cosine. The angle between the vectors is therefore defined by θ=arccos⁡u⋅v∥u∥∥v∥.\begin{equation} \label{eq:12:angle} \theta=\arccos\frac{\vv u\cdot\vv v}{\norm{\vv u}\norm{\vv v}}. \end{equation}θ=arccos∥u∥∥v∥u⋅v​.(12.2) For vectors in the coordinate plane or space, this agrees with the usual geometric angle: expand ∥u−v∥2\norm{\vv u-\vv v}^2∥u−v∥2 and compare with the school law of cosines. Its value is measured in radians. An angle with a zero vector is undefined. Two nonzero vectors are parallel if one is a scalar multiple of the other. They have the same direction if that scalar is positive. They have opposite directions if that scalar is negative. The corresponding angles are 0 and π\piπ. For the vectors (3,4,12)(3,4,12)(3,4,12) and (2,−1,2)(2,-1,2)(2,−1,2), the angle is arccos⁡(26/(13⋅3))=arccos⁡(2/3)\arccos(26/(13\cdot3))=\arccos(2/3)arccos(26/(13⋅3))=arccos(2/3).

Theorem 12.5 (Triangle inequality). For u,v∈Rn\vv u,\vv v\in\R^nu,v∈Rn, ∥u+v∥≤∥u∥+∥v∥.\norm{\vv u+\vv v}\leq\norm{\vv u}+\norm{\vv v}.∥u+v∥≤∥u∥+∥v∥. Equality holds exactly when one vector is zero or the two nonzero vectors have the same direction.

Writing u=λv\vv u=\lambda\vv vu=λv shows that the required equality is λ∥v∥2=∣λ∣∥v∥2\lambda\norm{\vv v}^2=|\lambda|\norm{\vv v}^2λ∥v∥2=∣λ∣∥v∥2. For the opposite pair a\vv aa and b\vv bb above, the sum is (2,−1)(2,-1)(2,−1). Its length is 5\sqrt55​, strictly less than the sum 353\sqrt535​ of their lengths. Thus the equality conditions for these two inequalities differ. Applied to b−a=(b−c)+(c−a)\vv b-\vv a=(\vv b-\vv c)+(\vv c-\vv a)b−a=(b−c)+(c−a), the triangle inequality says that going through a third point cannot shorten the direct distance. The same inequality also bounds changes in length. Apply it to u=(u−v)+v\vv u=(\vv u-\vv v)+\vv vu=(u−v)+v to obtain ∥u∥−∥v∥≤∥u−v∥\norm{\vv u}-\norm{\vv v}\leq\norm{\vv u-\vv v}∥u∥−∥v∥≤∥u−v∥. 12.5 Orthogonality and projection

Two vectors are orthogonal if their dot product is zero. For nonzero vectors, this means their angle is π/2\pi/2π/2. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, even though it has no angle. Expanding the square as above shows the Pythagorean identity: p⋅w=0⟹∥p+w∥2=∥p∥2+∥w∥2.\vv p\cdot\vv w=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \norm{\vv p+\vv w}^2=\norm{\vv p}^2+\norm{\vv w}^2.p⋅w=0⟹∥p+w∥2=∥p∥2+∥w∥2.

For a nonzero vector v\vv vv, the set L={tv:t∈R}L=\{t\vv v:t\in\R\}L={tv:t∈R} is the line through the origin in its direction. The orthogonal projection of u\vv uu onto this line is a vector p∈L\vv p\in Lp∈L such that u−p\vv u-\vv pu−p is orthogonal to v\vv vv. Figure 12.1 shows the two parts of the displacement.

Set w=u−αv\vv w=\vv u-\alpha\vv vw=u−αv. For any t∈Rt\in\Rt∈R, u−tv=w+(α−t)v\vv u-t\vv v=\vv w+(\alpha-t)\vv vu−tv=w+(α−t)v is a sum of orthogonal vectors. 12.6 Coordinates along perpendicular axes

An orthonormal pair consists of two orthogonal unit vectors. In the plane, take real numbers aaa and bbb with a2+b2=1a^2+b^2=1a2+b2=1 and set q1=(a,b),q2=(−b,a).\vv q_1=(a,b),\qquad \vv q_2=(-b,a).q1​=(a,b),q2​=(−b,a). Their squared norms are both a2+b2=1a^2+b^2=1a2+b2=1, and their dot product is −ab+ba=0-ab+ba=0−ab+ba=0. Thus they form an orthonormal pair. For any u=(x,y)\vv u=(x,y)u=(x,y), its signed components along these two directions are c1=u⋅q1=ax+by,c2=u⋅q2=−bx+ay.c_1=\vv u\cdot\vv q_1=ax+by,\qquad c_2=\vv u\cdot\vv q_2=-bx+ay.c1​=u⋅q1​=ax+by,c2​=u⋅q2​=−bx+ay. The corresponding vector sum is exactly u\vv uu, because c1q1+c2q2=((a2+b2)x,(a2+b2)y)=(x,y).c_1\vv q_1+c_2\vv q_2 =\bigl((a^2+b^2)x,(a^2+b^2)y\bigr)=(x,y).c1​q1​+c2​q2​=((a2+b2)x,(a2+b2)y)=(x,y). Summary

  • Vectors in Rn\R^nRn are ordered real lists. Addition and scalar multiplication act component by component. The standard coordinate representation (12.1) is unique.
  • Definition 12.2: the dot product is symmetric and bilinear. The Euclidean norm is nonnegative, vanishes only at zero and obeys ∥λu∥=∣λ∣∥u∥\norm{\lambda\vv u}=|\lambda|\norm{\vv u}∥λu∥=∣λ∣∥u∥.
  • Theorems 12.4 and 12.5: Cauchy–Schwarz has equality for dependent pairs. The triangle inequality has equality only for a zero vector or nonzero vectors in the same direction.
  • Equation (12.2) defines an angle only for two nonzero vectors. Orthogonality means zero dot product and includes the zero vector.
  • Theorem 12.6: projection onto a nonzero direction gives an orthogonal decomposition and the unique closest point on its line. The signed scalar component differs from its absolute length.
