Lecture 14

Lines, planes and coordinates

A line and a plane can be described by parameters or by scalar equations. We classify their intersections and compute angles and distances using normal components. Coordinate systems adapted to an axis then describe the same points by lengths and angles. Their parameter ranges must record the places where the description is not unique.

Uses.

  • Summary 2: Standing fact E supplies trigonometric identities and the restricted inverse trigonometric functions.

  • Summary 12: Norms measure distances. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds dot products, which give angles and orthogonal decompositions with unique closest points.

  • Summary 13: The cross product gives an oriented normal. Independent plane directions give equivalent parametric and normal equations. Convention C fixes right-handed spatial axes. The vector triple-product identity has its full component proof on Sheet 13.

Summary

  • A line has a nonzero direction. Substituting it into a plane’s normal equation gives one intersection, a contained line or no intersection, according to the scalar coefficient and constant.
  • Equation (14.3) and Theorem 14.1: the line–plane angle uses an absolute normal component and arcsine. The closest plane point is obtained by removing the signed normal displacement.
  • Independent plane normals give an intersection line in their cross-product direction. Proportional normals give coincident or disjoint planes.
  • Equations (14.4) and (14.5): cylindrical rrr is distance from the third axis, while spherical ρ\rhoρ is distance from the origin. The angle θ\thetaθ is polar and φ\varphiφ is azimuth.
  • A half-open azimuth range removes periodic duplication off the axis. It leaves an angular seam. The origin and axis retain the specified non-uniqueness of angles.

The polar curve (14.6) is an ellipse, with a circle as its ε=0\varepsilon=0ε=0 case. The sign check after squaring is essential. Sheet 14 treats parameter cases, skew lines and coordinate regions, and derives a polar equation for a centred ellipse. Lecture 15 gives a multiplication to the coordinate plane to construct complex numbers and describe rotations.
