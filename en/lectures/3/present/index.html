Lecture 3

Sequence limits and their algebra

We give a precise meaning to convergence of a sequence. An error estimate will turn a requested accuracy into an index after which every term has that accuracy. We use these estimates to prove the laws for sums, products and quotients of convergent sequences.

Uses.

  • Summary 1: Negating a quantified statement reverses each quantifier. The negation of P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q is PPP and not QQQ.

  • Summary 2: A function assigns one value in its codomain to each input in its domain. For h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1 and n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N, Bernoulli’s inequality gives (1+h)n≥1+nh(1+h)^n\geq 1+nh(1+h)n≥1+nh. The triangle estimates give ∣x+y∣≤∣x∣+∣y∣|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|∣x+y∣≤∣x∣+∣y∣ and ∣∣x∣−∣y∣∣≤∣x−y∣\bigl||x|-|y|\bigr|\leq|x-y|​∣x∣−∣y∣​≤∣x−y∣. For each x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R, the Archimedean property supplies N∈NN\in\NN∈N with N>xN>xN>x.

3.1 The ε\varepsilonε–NNN definition

A real sequence is a function from N\NN to R\RR. We write its value at n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N as ana_nan​ and call it the nnnth term. The sequence (an)(a_n)(an​) records the terms in their order. For example, the sequence given by an=1/na_n=1/nan​=1/n begins with 111, 1/21/21/2, 1/31/31/3 and 1/41/41/4.

The terms of this sequence become close to zero. A convergence statement must say how close they become and whether they stay close. We measure the error relative to a proposed limit a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R by ∣an−a∣|a_n-a|∣an​−a∣. An accuracy ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 specifies the open interval (a−ε,a+ε).(a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon).(a−ε,a+ε). A term lies in this interval exactly when its error is less than ε\varepsilonε.

Definition 3.1 (Convergence). A real sequence (an)(a_n)(an​) converges to a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R if ∀ε>0∃N∈N∀n∈N:n≥N ⟹ ∣an−a∣<ε.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:limit} \forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists N\in\N\quad \forall n\in\N:\quad n\geq N\ \Longrightarrow\ |a_n-a|<\varepsilon. \end{equation}∀ε>0∃N∈N∀n∈N:n≥N ⟹ ∣an​−a∣<ε.(3.1) We write an→aa_n\to aan​→a. A sequence is convergent if it converges to some a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R. A sequence that is not convergent is divergent.

For an=1/na_n=1/nan​=1/n, an accuracy of ε=1/4\varepsilon=1/4ε=1/4 is met by every term with n≥5n\geq5n≥5. Figure 3.1 shows this choice of NNN. The term at n=4n=4n=4 lies on the boundary. It fails the strict error condition.

A fixed band tests only one accuracy. Convergence requires the same kind of statement for every ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0. A smaller ε\varepsilonε may require a larger NNN. The index NNN may depend on ε\varepsilonε and on the sequence. It must be fixed before the index n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N is chosen.

We say that a property holds eventually if it holds for every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N for some N∈NN\in\NN∈N. Thus convergence to aaa means that each positive error tolerance is met eventually. The earlier terms need not meet it.

Negating the definition

The negation rules of Summary 1 give an exact test for failure of convergence to a specified a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R: ∃ε0>0∀N∈N∃n∈N:n≥N and ∣an−a∣≥ε0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:negation} \exists\varepsilon_0>0\quad\forall N\in\N\quad \exists n\in\N:\quad n\geq N\ \text{and}\ |a_n-a|\geq\varepsilon_0. \end{equation}∃ε0​>0∀N∈N∃n∈N:n≥N and ∣an​−a∣≥ε0​.(3.2) Negating the implication gives n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N together with failure of the error condition. Negating the strict error bound gives ∣an−a∣≥ε0|a_n-a|\geq\varepsilon_0∣an​−a∣≥ε0​.

The tolerance ε0\varepsilon_0ε0​ is fixed. Every proposed cutoff has at least one term with index n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N that fails this tolerance. Those failing terms may depend on NNN. They need not be all the later terms.

For example, the sequence given by an=(−1)na_n=(-1)^nan​=(−1)n does not converge to zero. Take ε0=1\varepsilon_0=1ε0​=1. For any N∈NN\in\NN∈N, the choice n=Nn=Nn=N gives ∣an−0∣=1|a_n-0|=1∣an​−0∣=1. This disproves convergence to zero. To prove that the sequence is divergent, we must rule out every proposed a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R. We will do this in §3.3.

3.3 Uniqueness and boundedness

A sequence (an)(a_n)(an​) is bounded if there is M>0M>0M>0 such that ∣an∣≤Mfor every n∈N.|a_n|\leq M\qquad\text{for every }n\in\N.∣an​∣≤Mfor every n∈N. The bound controls all terms, including any early exceptions to an error tolerance. Boundedness does not specify a limit.

Theorem 3.5 (Uniqueness and boundedness). Every convergent real sequence has a unique limit and is bounded.

Proof of uniqueness. We argue by contradiction. Suppose an→aa_n\to aan​→a and an→ba_n\to ban​→b, where a≠ba\ne ba=b. Define d=∣a−b∣>0d=|a-b|>0d=∣a−b∣>0. Choose n≥max⁡{Na,Nb}n\geq\max\{N_a,N_b\}n≥max{Na​,Nb​}. Both estimates hold at this same index. By the triangle inequality from Summary 2, d=∣a−b∣≤∣a−an∣+∣an−b∣<d3+d3=2d3.d=|a-b|\leq|a-a_n|+|a_n-b|<\frac d3+\frac d3=\frac{2d}3.d=∣a−b∣≤∣a−an​∣+∣an​−b∣<3d​+3d​=32d​. This contradicts d>0d>0d>0. Hence a=ba=ba=b. 

We may therefore write the limit as lim⁡n→∞an=a\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=alimn→∞​an​=a.

Proof of boundedness. Suppose an→aa_n\to aan​→a. With ε=1\varepsilon=1ε=1, the definition supplies N∈NN\in\NN∈N such that ∣an−a∣<1|a_n-a|<1∣an​−a∣<1 for every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N. The tolerance 1 supplies a bound for the entire later part of the sequence. It does not need to be arbitrarily small for this purpose. The finite maximum includes the earlier terms, which convergence alone does not control. A bound on only the later terms would not yet be the bound asserted in the theorem.

Boundedness does not imply convergence

The sequence given by an=(−1)na_n=(-1)^nan​=(−1)n is bounded, since ∣an∣=1|a_n|=1∣an​∣=1. We show that it has no limit. Suppose it converged to some a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R. With ε=1\varepsilon=1ε=1, convergence would give N∈NN\in\NN∈N such that ∣an−a∣<1|a_n-a|<1∣an​−a∣<1 for all n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N.

Choose an even index m≥Nm\geq Nm≥N and an odd index k≥Nk\geq Nk≥N. These choices are possible after every cutoff. Then am=1a_m=1am​=1 and ak=−1a_k=-1ak​=−1, so both ∣1−a∣<1|1-a|<1∣1−a∣<1 and ∣a−(−1)∣<1|a-(-1)|<1∣a−(−1)∣<1. Thus a bounded sequence may be divergent.

3.4 The algebra of limits

A direct error proof establishes a limit for one sequence. To combine sequences, we need to control several errors at the same indices. If one estimate holds for n≥N1n\geq N_1n≥N1​ and another for n≥N2n\geq N_2n≥N2​, both hold for every n≥max⁡{N1,N2}n\geq\max\{N_1,N_2\}n≥max{N1​,N2​}. Their cutoffs may differ. The maximum gives a single cutoff for the combined argument.

Changing finitely many terms does not change a limit. To check this, fix ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 and take an original error cutoff. Increase it past all changed indices. Every remaining error is unchanged and is less than ε\varepsilonε. This observation also lets us fill any finitely many undefined initial positions of a formula with arbitrary real values. The convergence assertion concerns the resulting sequence.

Theorem 3.6 (Limit laws). Let an→aa_n\to aan​→a and bn→bb_n\to bbn​→b be real sequences, and let c∈Rc\in\Rc∈R. Then an+bn→a+b,can→ca,anbn→ab.a_n+b_n\to a+b,\qquad ca_n\to ca,\qquad a_nb_n\to ab.an​+bn​→a+b,can​→ca,an​bn​→ab. If b≠0b\ne0b=0, then bn≠0b_n\ne0bn​=0 eventually and anbn⟶ab.\frac{a_n}{b_n}\longrightarrow\frac ab.bn​an​​⟶ba​. Undefined initial quotients may be assigned arbitrary real values.

Proof of the sum law. Let ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 be given. Choose Na∈NN_a\in\NNa​∈N such that ∣an−a∣<ε/2|a_n-a|<\varepsilon/2∣an​−a∣<ε/2 for every n≥Nan\geq N_an≥Na​. Choose Nb∈NN_b\in\NNb​∈N such that ∣bn−b∣<ε/2|b_n-b|<\varepsilon/2∣bn​−b∣<ε/2 for every n≥Nbn\geq N_bn≥Nb​. Put N=max⁡{Na,Nb}N=\max\{N_a,N_b\}N=max{Na​,Nb​}. Then check every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N: ∣(an+bn)−(a+b)∣≤∣an−a∣+∣bn−b∣<ε2+ε2=ε.|(a_n+b_n)-(a+b)| \leq|a_n-a|+|b_n-b|<\frac\varepsilon2+\frac\varepsilon2 =\varepsilon.∣(an​+bn​)−(a+b)∣≤∣an​−a∣+∣bn​−b∣<2ε​+2ε​=ε. This is convergence of the sum to a+ba+ba+b. 

Each summand is controlled at the smaller tolerance ε/2\varepsilon/2ε/2. Using tolerance ε\varepsilonε for each would give only a total error below 2ε2\varepsilon2ε. The definition allows a choice at any positive tolerance, so it allows the two choices used in the proof.

Proof of the product law. By Theorem 3.5, choose M>0M>0M>0 with ∣an∣≤M|a_n|\leq M∣an​∣≤M for every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N. Subtracting the proposed product limit gives the identity anbn−ab=an(bn−b)+b(an−a).a_nb_n-ab=a_n(b_n-b)+b(a_n-a).an​bn​−ab=an​(bn​−b)+b(an​−a). This writes the error as a sum involving the two known errors. Let ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 be given and put δ=ε/(M+∣b∣+1)>0\delta=\varepsilon/(M+|b|+1)>0δ=ε/(M+∣b∣+1)>0. Choose N∈NN\in\NN∈N such that both ∣an−a∣<δ|a_n-a|<\delta∣an​−a∣<δ and ∣bn−b∣<δ|b_n-b|<\delta∣bn​−b∣<δ hold for every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N. Such an NNN is the maximum of the two convergence cutoffs. Then check every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N: ∣anbn−ab∣≤M∣bn−b∣+∣b∣∣an−a∣≤(M+∣b∣)δ<ε.|a_nb_n-ab|\leq M|b_n-b|+|b||a_n-a| \leq(M+|b|)\delta<\varepsilon.∣an​bn​−ab∣≤M∣bn​−b∣+∣b∣∣an​−a∣≤(M+∣b∣)δ<ε. The last inequality uses M+∣b∣<M+∣b∣+1M+|b|<M+|b|+1M+∣b∣<M+∣b∣+1. This proves the product law. 

The bound MMM controls the coefficient ana_nan​ in the error identity. Without that control, making ∣bn−b∣|b_n-b|∣bn​−b∣ small would not yet control an(bn−b)a_n(b_n-b)an​(bn​−b). Every constant sequence with value ccc converges to ccc, since its error is zero. Applying the product law to ana_nan​ and that constant sequence proves the scalar-multiple law. It also covers c=0c=0c=0. Replacing ccc by −1-1−1 and using the sum law gives the corresponding law for differences.

Proof of the quotient law. Suppose b≠0b\ne0b=0. Convergence with tolerance ∣b∣/2|b|/2∣b∣/2 supplies N0∈NN_0\in\NN0​∈N such that ∣bn−b∣<∣b∣/2|b_n-b|<|b|/2∣bn​−b∣<∣b∣/2 for every n≥N0n\geq N_0n≥N0​. Let ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 be given. In addition, choose a convergence cutoff for ∣bn−b∣<ε∣b∣2/2|b_n-b|<\varepsilon|b|^2/2∣bn​−b∣<ε∣b∣2/2. Take NNN to be the maximum of that cutoff and N0N_0N0​. The first cutoff keeps the denominator away from zero. The second controls the requested reciprocal error. A denominator can have a nonzero limit without being nonzero at every early index. Its finitely many early zeros do not enter the proof after the two cutoffs have been combined.

Summary

  • Definition 3.1: for every ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, choose N∈NN\in\NN∈N so that ∣an−a∣<ε|a_n-a|<\varepsilon∣an​−a∣<ε for every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N.
  • Equation (3.2): failure to converge to a specified a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R means that some fixed ε0>0\varepsilon_0>0ε0​>0 fails at a term with index n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N for every N∈NN\in\NN∈N.
  • The limits 1/n→01/n\to01/n→0, (2n+1)/(n+3)→2(2n+1)/(n+3)\to2(2n+1)/(n+3)→2 and qn→0q^n\to0qn→0 for real ∣q∣<1|q|<1∣q∣<1 follow from explicit error bounds.
  • A property holds eventually if it holds for all n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N for some N∈NN\in\NN∈N. A real sequence is bounded if ∣an∣≤M|a_n|\leq M∣an​∣≤M for some M>0M>0M>0 and every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N.
  • Theorem 3.5: a convergent real sequence has a unique limit and is bounded. Both assertions are proved here.
  • Theorem 3.6: sums, scalar multiples and products preserve limits. Quotients preserve limits when the denominator limit is nonzero.

Boundedness alone does not imply convergence. Neither do successive differences tending to zero. The limit laws use one cutoff for several errors. A quotient requires a nonzero denominator limit. Lecture 4 will use order and completeness to prove existence of further limits.
