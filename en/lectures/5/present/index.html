Lecture 5

Function limits and continuity

A function limit controls values near an input rather than terms after an index. We formulate that control by a distance threshold and connect it with sequences. Continuity then compares a nearby limit with the function’s actual value. The domain determines which inputs are tested.

Uses.

  • Summary 1: The number 2\sqrt 22​ is irrational. Quantifier negation reverses the choices.

  • Summary 2: Functions have specified domains. The triangle and reverse-triangle inequalities control errors. Rational numbers are dense in R\RR. The Archimedean property makes 1/n1/n1/n arbitrarily small.

  • Summary 3: A sequence converges when every positive error tolerance holds eventually. Real sequence limits are unique and obey the algebraic laws.

  • Summary 4: Order passes to limits. The squeeze theorem proves a limit from two agreeing bounds.

  • Standing fact E: School domains, algebraic identities and radian trigonometric bounds are assumed.

5.3 Directions and infinite limits

A one-sided limit tests only the specified side of the input. The point must be a cluster point from that side: every radius must contain a domain input strictly on the specified side. A limit at +∞+\infty+∞ requires a domain unbounded above. At −∞-\infty−∞ it requires one unbounded below. The following table gives the input conditions replacing the deleted condition at a finite point. The cutoff δ\deltaδ or RRR is positive.

Infinity is not a real number to substitute into an algebraic limit law.

A two-sided limit persists on either restricted side having the point as a cluster point: its same radius still controls every tested input. Conversely, if both side limits exist and agree, choose the smaller of their radii for each requested error or bound. Using positive bounds MMM is equivalent to using every real bound: a positive bound larger than a requested real bound gives its test too.

The sequential characterisation also holds for each approach in the table. For a finite output limit, the forward proof uses the input sequence’s cutoff for the chosen radius or large-input bound. If the limit fails, a fixed tolerance fails at an input for every cutoff. They form an allowed approach sequence whose outputs fail that tolerance. For an infinite output limit, the same argument uses a fixed failed output bound instead of a tolerance. Consequently the finite-output arithmetic and order laws hold at these approaches too, by applying the sequence laws to every approach sequence. No law here treats infinity as a real output value.

5.4 Continuity and its domain

Definition 5.8 (Relative continuity). For f:A→Rf:A\to\Rf:A→R and c∈Ac\in Ac∈A, continuity at ccc means ∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x∈A:∣x−c∣<δ ⟹ ∣f(x)−f(c)∣<ε.\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists\delta>0\quad\forall x\in A: \quad |x-c|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-f(c)|<\varepsilon.∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x∈A:∣x−c∣<δ ⟹ ∣f(x)−f(c)∣<ε. The function is continuous on AAA if it is continuous at every point of AAA.

The input x=cx=cx=c is now allowed and has error zero. At a cluster point in the domain, continuity is exactly equality of the deleted limit with f(c)f(c)f(c). At an isolated domain point, choose a radius containing no other domain point. The function is automatically continuous there. At the endpoint of a closed interval, only the available one-sided inputs are tested. Continuity does not require inputs outside the stated domain.

Proposition 5.9 (Sequential continuity). A function f:A→Rf:A\to\Rf:A→R is continuous at c∈Ac\in Ac∈A exactly when every sequence xn∈Ax_n\in Axn​∈A converging to ccc satisfies f(xn)→f(c)f(x_n)\to f(c)f(xn​)→f(c).

Proof. The forward implication follows by choosing the continuity radius for a requested error, then an input-sequence cutoff for that radius. Conversely, failure of continuity supplies one ε0>0\varepsilon_0>0ε0​>0 and, for each nnn, an xn∈Ax_n\in Axn​∈A with ∣xn−c∣<1/n|x_n-c|<1/n∣xn​−c∣<1/n and ∣f(xn)−f(c)∣≥ε0|f(x_n)-f(c)|\geq\varepsilon_0∣f(xn​)−f(c)∣≥ε0​. These inputs tend to ccc, while their images do not tend to f(c)f(c)f(c), contradicting the stated property. This proof also covers isolated points. There the proposed failure cannot supply any such input. 

Constants and the identity function are continuous: their errors are, respectively, zero and ∣x−c∣|x-c|∣x−c∣. The algebraic laws therefore prove continuity of all polynomials and of rational functions wherever their denominators do not vanish. If ggg is continuous at ccc, fff is continuous at g(c)g(c)g(c) and ggg maps its domain into the domain of fff, then f∘gf\circ gf∘g is continuous at ccc. Indeed, an input sequence approaching ccc has inner images approaching g(c)g(c)g(c). Outer continuity then gives the required composite image limit. This remains valid when some inner images equal g(c)g(c)g(c).

Standing fact E assumes that the school exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric functions are continuous on their real domains. This uses the relative definition, including allowed domain endpoints. For a fixed r∈Rr\in\Rr∈R, continuity of xrx^rxr on x>0x>0x>0 follows from xr=exp⁡(rln⁡x)x^r=\exp(r\ln x)xr=exp(rlnx) and continuous composition. For r>0r>0r>0, define 0r=00^r=00r=0. This gives continuity also at zero from the nonnegative domain: given ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, take δ=ε1/r\delta=\varepsilon^{1/r}δ=ε1/r. Order of logarithms and exponentials shows that 0<x<δ0<x<\delta0<x<δ implies 0<xr<δr=ε0<x^r<\delta^r=\varepsilon0<xr<δr=ε. The input zero has error zero. Thus this endpoint continuity is proved, rather than added to the standing assumption.

A removable discontinuity at a domain cluster point has a finite limit different from its assigned value. For instance, assigning the previous cancelled quotient the value 100 at 1 gives limit 2 but failure of continuity. Reassigning the value 2 repairs it. A jump discontinuity has two distinct finite one-sided limits. The function equal to −1-1−1 for x<0x<0x<0 and 1 for x≥0x\geq0x≥0 has a jump at zero. An infinite discontinuity has an infinite limit from at least one available side. The function 1/x1/x1/x, assigned any real value at zero, is an example. Oscillation can prevent both finite and infinite limits, as sin⁡(1/x)\sin(1/x)sin(1/x) at zero shows. At a cluster point absent from a function’s domain, assigning a value there is an extension problem. A continuous extension is possible exactly when a finite deleted limit exists there.

A continuous extension at a missing cluster point is unique when it exists. If the deleted limit is LLL, assign that value at the point. The existing radius controls all other inputs and the new point has error zero, proving continuity. Conversely, continuity would make the assigned value equal to the unique deleted limit.

5.5 The basic sine limit

Summary

  • Definition 5.1 and Theorem 5.3: deleted neighbourhood limits at cluster points are equivalent to limits along every allowed approaching sequence.
  • Theorems 5.5 and 5.7: arithmetic, order and squeeze laws hold. Composition must handle inner values equal to the outer point.
  • One-sided and infinite limits change the input tests or output bounds, with the same order of tolerance and cutoff choices.
  • Definition 5.8 and Proposition 5.9: continuity is relative to the domain and includes the actual value at the point.
  • Theorem 5.12: sin⁡x/x→1\sin x/x\to1sinx/x→1 in radians, using the stated school bounds and continuity assumption E.
