Lecture 13

Oriented geometry and work

The dot product measures a component along a direction. The cross product records an area together with a choice of normal direction. We use these products in stated models for work and torque, then calculate signed volumes. Their signs depend on a fixed orientation of the coordinate axes.

Uses.

  • Summary 2: Standing fact E supplies the trigonometric identities and radians.

  • Summary 6: The proved limits are sin⁡h/h→1\sin h/h\to 1sinh/h→1 and (cos⁡h−1)/h→0(\cos h-1)/h\to 0(cosh−1)/h→0 as h→0h\to 0h→0. Finite sums preserve limits.

  • Summary 12: Vector operations are componentwise. The dot product is bilinear, defines the norm and the angle, and gives orthogonal projection. Cauchy–Schwarz has equality exactly for dependent pairs. Independence means that a zero linear combination has only zero coefficients.

13.1 The cross product and orientation

An ordered set of three mutually perpendicular unit axes has a choice of orientation. The axes are right-handed when curling the right-hand fingers from the first positive axis towards the second makes the thumb point along the third positive axis.

Convention C.

Spatial Cartesian axes are right-handed throughout this course. The cross product uses this orientation. Coordinate-angle and boundary conventions will be specified when those objects are introduced.

13.2 Work and torque in a stated force model

A force vector specifies a force’s components, measured in newtons. We use the following mechanical models as physical premises. For a constant force F\vv FF and a net displacement d\vv dd, the work is W=F⋅d.\begin{equation} \label{eq:13:work} W=\vv F\cdot\vv d. \end{equation}W=F⋅d.(13.2) Work is a scalar, measured in joules when displacement is in metres. One joule is one newton metre. For nonzero displacement, this is the signed force component in its direction, F⋅(d/∥d∥)\vv F\cdot(\vv d/\norm{\vv d})F⋅(d/∥d∥), multiplied by ∥d∥\norm{\vv d}∥d∥. Equivalently, for two nonzero vectors the work is ∥F∥∥d∥cos⁡θ\norm{\vv F}\norm{\vv d}\cos\theta∥F∥∥d∥cosθ. Work is negative when the force component opposes the displacement. A zero force or zero displacement gives zero work without defining an angle. The constant-force endpoint formula is the model here. Lecture 40 treats forces that vary along a path.

Fix a reference point OOO. Let r\vv rr be the displacement from OOO to the point where a force F\vv FF acts. The torque about OOO in the force model is τ=r×F.\begin{equation} \label{eq:13:torque} \vv\tau=\vv r\times\vv F. \end{equation}τ=r×F.(13.3) Torque is a vector measured in newton metres. It depends on the chosen reference point. For nonzero r\vv rr and F\vv FF, its magnitude is ∥r∥∥F∥sin⁡θ\norm{\vv r}\norm{\vv F}\sin\theta∥r∥∥F∥sinθ. The line of action of a nonzero force is the line through its application point in direction F\vv FF. The distance from OOO to this line is the lever arm. Lecture 12’s projection decomposition gives its length as ∥r∥sin⁡θ\norm{\vv r}\sin\theta∥r∥sinθ. Thus torque magnitude is force magnitude times lever arm. If r=0\vv r=\vv0r=0, the lever arm and torque are zero. A force whose line of action goes through OOO also has zero torque.

13.5 Order and parentheses

A cross product produces a vector, so another cross product can be taken. The placement of parentheses matters. Summary

  • Definition 13.1 and Theorem 13.2: in right-handed axes, the cross product is bilinear and antisymmetric, is orthogonal to its two arguments, and has magnitude equal to their parallelogram area. It vanishes exactly for a dependent pair.
  • Equations (13.2) and (13.3): the stated models give constant-force work F⋅d\vv F\cdot\vv dF⋅d and torque about a reference point r×F\vv r\times\vv Fr×F. The axial torque is the limiting work per unit small rotation about that axis, as proved for the third coordinate axis.
  • Equation (13.4) and Theorem 13.6: the scalar triple product is a signed volume. It is cyclically invariant, changes sign under an exchange, and vanishes exactly for coplanar vectors.
  • Equations (13.5) and (13.6): independent plane directions give a nonzero cross-product normal. Parametric and normal equations describe the same plane.
  • Cross products require their argument order and parentheses. The cross product is not associative.

Sheet 13 practices areas, mechanical signs and degenerate volumes, then proves the additional vector identity. Lecture 14 uses normal directions to describe lines, planes and their intersections.
