Statements, sets and proofs

What this lecture does

A mathematical claim specifies the objects it concerns and the property it asserts. We learn to read those specifications, negate them and prove them. The same rules will govern the definitions of a limit and continuity.

1.1 Statements and their domains

A statement is a claim that is either true or false. The sentence “the integer 12 is divisible by 3” is a statement. The expression x2>1x^2>1x2>1 does not yet specify a statement. Its truth depends on the number assigned to xxx. For x=2x=2x=2 it is true. For x=0x=0x=0 it is false.

The domain specifies which objects a variable may denote. Throughout these notes, the natural numbers and integers are denoted by N={1,2,3,…},Z={…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…}.\N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\},\qquad \Z=\{\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\}.N={1,2,3,…},Z={…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…}. The symbol Q\QQ denotes the rational numbers. A rational number has the form p/qp/qp/q, where p∈Zp\in\Zp∈Z and q∈Nq\in\Nq∈N. The symbol R\RR denotes the real numbers. We use their school arithmetic. Lecture 2 states the additional property of R\RR that makes the existence of limits possible.

A set is a collection of objects, called its elements. The notation x∈Ax\in Ax∈A means that xxx belongs to the set AAA. The notation x∉Ax\notin Ax∈/A means that it does not. The empty set, denoted by ∅\varnothing∅, has no elements. For instance, the set A={x∈R:x2=1}A=\{x\in\R:x^2=1\}A={x∈R:x2=1} has the elements −1-1−1 and 111. The colon means “such that”. The displayed condition determines which real numbers belong to AAA.

The same formula can make different claims on different domains. Every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N satisfies n2≥nn^2\geq nn2≥n. The assertion that every x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R satisfies x2≥xx^2\geq xx2≥x is false, since x=1/2x=1/2x=1/2 violates it. A proof must use only the assumptions stated for its domain. A counterexample must belong to that domain.

We write A⊆BA\subseteq BA⊆B if every element of AAA is an element of BBB. This is called inclusion. Equality of sets means that they have the same elements. To prove A=BA=BA=B, it is enough to prove A⊆BA\subseteq BA⊆B and B⊆AB\subseteq AB⊆A. This method requires two implications. Checking one of them proves only an inclusion.

Intervals specify common real domains. For a<ba<ba<b, we use the notation (a,b)={x∈R:a<x<b},[a,b]={x∈R:a≤x≤b}.(a,b)=\{x\in\R:a<x<b\},\qquad [a,b]=\{x\in\R:a\leq x\leq b\}.(a,b)={x∈R:a<x<b},[a,b]={x∈R:a≤x≤b}. A round endpoint is excluded. A square endpoint is included. The intervals (a,b](a,b](a,b] and [a,b)[a,b)[a,b) include just one endpoint. An interval such as (a,∞)(a,\infty)(a,∞) means {x∈R:x>a}\{x\in\R:x>a\}{x∈R:x>a}, and [a,∞)[a,\infty)[a,∞) includes aaa. Infinity is not a real endpoint or an element that the interval contains.

1.2 Combining statements

Let PPP and QQQ be statements. Their conjunction, written P∧QP\land QP∧Q, means that PPP and QQQ are both true. Their disjunction, written P∨QP\lor QP∨Q, means that at least one is true. Mathematical “or” includes the case where both are true. The negation ¬P\neg P¬P is true exactly when PPP is false.

An inequality illustrates the difference between a claim and its negation. For x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R, the negation of x<2x<2x<2 is x≥2x\geq2x≥2. It includes equality. The assertion x>2x>2x>2 excludes equality, so it is not the negation of x<2x<2x<2. This endpoint distinction will matter when we negate a strict error bound.

An implication, written P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q, asserts that whenever PPP is true, QQQ is true. The statement PPP is its hypothesis. The statement QQQ is its conclusion. A failure of the implication means that its hypothesis holds but its conclusion fails. Its truth values are therefore given by

PPP QQQ P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q
true true true
true false false
false true true
false false true

For example, for every x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R the following implication holds: x>2 ⟹ x2>4.x>2\ \Longrightarrow\ x^2>4.x>2 ⟹ x2>4. A number x≤2x\leq2x≤2 cannot refute it. At such a number its hypothesis does not hold. To refute the implication, we would need a real number that is larger than 2 and whose square is at most 4. No such number exists: if x>2x>2x>2, then both x−2x-2x−2 and x+2x+2x+2 are positive, so x2−4=(x−2)(x+2)>0x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)>0x2−4=(x−2)(x+2)>0.

The converse of P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q is Q⇒PQ\Rightarrow PQ⇒P. The converse makes a different claim. The number x=−3x=-3x=−3 shows that x2>4x^2>4x2>4 does not imply x>2x>2x>2. Knowing that a square is large does not determine the sign of its base.

The contrapositive of P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q is ¬Q⇒¬P\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P¬Q⇒¬P. An implication and its contrapositive have the same truth value. Both fail exactly when PPP is true and QQQ is false. For the preceding example, the contrapositive is x2≤4 ⟹ x≤2.x^2\leq4\ \Longrightarrow\ x\leq2.x2≤4 ⟹ x≤2. It retains the original direction of reasoning in its logically equivalent form. It is not the converse.

An equivalence, written P⇔QP\Leftrightarrow QP⇔Q, asserts both P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q and Q⇒PQ\Rightarrow PQ⇒P. We also say “PPP if and only if QQQ”. Proving an equivalence requires both directions unless a chain of already justified equivalences establishes them together.

Proposition 1.1 (Negation rules). For statements PPP and QQQ, negation obeys the rules ¬(P∧Q) ⇔ (¬P)∨(¬Q),¬(P∨Q) ⇔ (¬P)∧(¬Q).\neg(P\land Q)\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\neg P)\lor(\neg Q),\qquad \neg(P\lor Q)\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\neg P)\land(\neg Q).¬(P∧Q) ⇔ (¬P)∨(¬Q),¬(P∨Q) ⇔ (¬P)∧(¬Q). The negation of P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q is P∧¬QP\land\neg QP∧¬Q.

Proof. The conjunction P∧QP\land QP∧Q fails exactly when at least one of PPP and QQQ is false. The disjunction P∨QP\lor QP∨Q fails exactly when both are false. The implication table has one false case: PPP is true and QQQ is false. These are the stated negations. 

For a fixed x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R, negating the condition 0<x<10<x<10<x<1 therefore gives x≤0x\leq0x≤0 or x≥1x\geq1x≥1. Negating the claim that an implication holds means retaining its hypothesis and negating its conclusion.

The same connectives define set operations. The union A∪BA\cup BA∪B contains the objects belonging to AAA or BBB. The intersection A∩BA\cap BA∩B contains the objects belonging to both. The difference A∖BA\setminus BA∖B contains the elements of AAA that do not belong to BBB. These definitions specify membership, so an identity between such sets can be proved by following an arbitrary element through its membership conditions.

1.3 Quantifiers and their negations

A universal quantifier asserts a property of every object in a domain. We write “for every x∈Ax\in Ax∈A” as ∀x∈A\forall x\in A∀x∈A. An existential quantifier asserts that at least one object in the domain has the property. We write “there exists x∈Ax\in Ax∈A” as ∃x∈A\exists x\in A∃x∈A. Existence does not assert uniqueness.

A proof of a universal statement must cover an arbitrary member of its domain. A proof of an existential statement can give one object and verify its property. We call such an object a witness. These are different proof obligations.

Example 1.2 (Order of quantifiers). We prove the statement ∀x∈R∃y∈R:y>x.\forall x\in\R\quad\exists y\in\R:\quad y>x.∀x∈R∃y∈R:y>x. Given x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R, choose y=x+1y=x+1y=x+1. This is a real number and satisfies y>xy>xy>x. The choice may depend on xxx, because xxx was given first.

Reversing the quantifiers gives the statement ∃y∈R∀x∈R:y>x.\exists y\in\R\quad\forall x\in\R:\quad y>x.∃y∈R∀x∈R:y>x. This statement is false. For any proposed y∈Ry\in\Ry∈R, the real number x=y+1x=y+1x=y+1 satisfies x>yx>yx>y. Thus no single yyy works for every xxx.

The order of choices is part of a quantified claim. In the first statement, the witness yyy is chosen after xxx is specified. In the second statement, it must be chosen before xxx is specified. The symbols alone do not record a particular formula for the witness. A proof supplies that formula or another justified construction.

Negating a universal statement requires a counterexample. Negating an existential statement requires excluding every possible witness. The quantifier rules are ¬(∀x∈A: P(x)) ⇔ ∃x∈A: ¬P(x),\begin{equation} \label{eq:1:quantifiers} \neg(\forall x\in A:\ P(x))\ \Leftrightarrow\ \exists x\in A:\ \neg P(x), \end{equation}¬(∀x∈A: P(x)) ⇔ ∃x∈A: ¬P(x),(1.1) ¬(∃x∈A: P(x)) ⇔ ∀x∈A: ¬P(x).\neg(\exists x\in A:\ P(x))\ \Leftrightarrow\ \forall x\in A:\ \neg P(x).¬(∃x∈A: P(x)) ⇔ ∀x∈A: ¬P(x). For a claim with several quantifiers, apply these rules in their original order. Each quantifier changes kind. The final property is negated too.

For instance, negating the first displayed statement of the example gives ∃x∈R∀y∈R:y≤x.\exists x\in\R\quad\forall y\in\R:\quad y\leq x.∃x∈R∀y∈R:y≤x. This would assert a real number at least as large as every real number. The statement is false, in agreement with the proof of the original claim. The relation changes from y>xy>xy>x to y≤xy\leq xy≤x, rather than to y<xy<xy<x.

The earlier implication about squares was asserted for every x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R. Its negation must therefore include an existential quantifier: ∃x∈R:x>2 and x2≤4.\exists x\in\R:\quad x>2\ \text{and}\ x^2\leq4.∃x∈R:x>2 and x2≤4. This asks for one real counterexample satisfying the hypothesis and failing the conclusion. Negating the implication alone describes the property that such a counterexample would have. Negating the universal claim also asserts that a counterexample exists.

A domain restriction must survive negation. The negation of “every x∈(0,1)x\in(0,1)x∈(0,1) satisfies x2<xx^2<xx2<x” asks for an x∈(0,1)x\in(0,1)x∈(0,1) with x2≥xx^2\geq xx2≥x. The number x=2x=2x=2 cannot be that witness because it lies outside the domain. When the domain is empty, a universal statement has no counterexample and is true. An existential statement over the empty set is false because it has no possible witness.

1.4 Direct proof and contraposition

A proof is an argument that derives a claim from its stated assumptions by valid steps. In a direct proof of P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q, we assume PPP and derive QQQ. We do not assume the conclusion we are trying to establish.

An integer nnn is even if n=2kn=2kn=2k for some k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z. It is odd if n=2k+1n=2k+1n=2k+1 for some k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z. Every integer is exactly one of even or odd, by division with remainder by 2. We use this school arithmetic fact.

Proposition 1.3 (Parity of a square). For n∈Zn\in\Zn∈Z, the integer n2n^2n2 is even if and only if nnn is even.

Proof. First suppose nnn is even. There exists k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z such that n=2kn=2kn=2k. Then n2=4k2=2(2k2)n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)n2=4k2=2(2k2). Since 2k2∈Z2k^2\in\Z2k2∈Z, this is the definition of an even integer.

For the converse, we prove the contrapositive. Suppose nnn is odd, so n=2k+1n=2k+1n=2k+1 for some k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z. Expansion gives n2=(2k+1)2=4k2+4k+1=2(2k2+2k)+1.n^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1.n2=(2k+1)2=4k2+4k+1=2(2k2+2k)+1. The number 2k2+2k2k^2+2k2k2+2k is an integer. Thus n2n^2n2 is odd. We have proved that an odd integer cannot have an even square. Hence an integer with an even square is even. 

The two directions use different arguments. In the direct direction, substituting the even form of nnn produces the even form of n2n^2n2. In the other direction, the odd form of nnn produces an odd square. The latter calculation proves a contrapositive and therefore proves the required implication.

A true converse cannot be inferred from a true implication without an argument. In this proposition it has an argument. The earlier example x>2⇒x2>4x>2\Rightarrow x^2>4x>2⇒x2>4 had a false converse. The difference lies in the properties being proved, not in a rule that reverses every implication.

A counterexample refutes a universal claim by satisfying its hypotheses and violating its conclusion. One example suffices to refute such a claim. Any finite list of successful examples proves only those instances. It does not cover all members of an infinite domain.

A proof can use an arbitrary element without assigning it a numerical value. For example, when nnn is an arbitrary even integer, the expression n=2kn=2kn=2k covers every even integer because it states their defining property. The calculation above is therefore universal. Checking only n=2n=2n=2, n=4n=4n=4 and n=6n=6n=6 would not have that effect.

1.5 Proof by contradiction

In a proof by contradiction, we assume the hypotheses and the negation of the desired conclusion. We derive an impossibility from these assumptions. Since the hypotheses are retained, the negated conclusion must fail.

We use positive square roots with their school meaning. The number 2\sqrt22​ is the positive real number whose square is 2.

Theorem 1.4 (Irrationality of 2\sqrt22​). The number 2\sqrt22​ is not rational.

Proof. We argue by contradiction. Suppose 2=p/q\sqrt2=p/q2​=p/q, where p∈Zp\in\Zp∈Z and q∈Nq\in\Nq∈N. By cancelling common factors, choose this representation with ppp and qqq having no common integer divisor greater than 1. Squaring the equality and multiplying by q2q^2q2 gives p2=2q2.p^2=2q^2.p2=2q2. Hence p2p^2p2 is even. By Proposition 1.3, ppp is even. Write p=2kp=2kp=2k with k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z. Substitution gives 4k2=2q2,q2=2k2.4k^2=2q^2,\qquad q^2=2k^2.4k2=2q2,q2=2k2. Thus q2q^2q2 is even. The same proposition implies that qqq is even. The integers ppp and qqq therefore share the divisor 2. This contradicts the chosen representation. Hence 2∉Q\sqrt2\notin\Q2​∈/Q. 

The contradiction is the common divisor of a fraction assumed to have none. The equality p2=2q2p^2=2q^2p2=2q2 is an intermediate consequence, rather than a contradiction by itself. Keeping track of the assumed properties of ppp and qqq identifies the final step.

A contradiction proof and a contrapositive proof are related but have different starting assumptions. To prove P⇒QP\Rightarrow QP⇒Q by contraposition, we assume ¬Q\neg Q¬Q and derive ¬P\neg P¬P. By contradiction, we assume both PPP and ¬Q\neg Q¬Q and derive an impossibility. Both establish the original implication.

The irrationality proof used the least-terms representation of a rational number and the parity proposition. It did not assume any decimal expansion of 2\sqrt22​. An approximation such as 1.414 determines neither the existence of a rational representation nor its impossibility. The proof addresses every proposed rational representation at once.

In later lectures, an existence claim and a uniqueness claim will often appear in the same theorem. They require separate arguments. Existence asks for at least one object satisfying the stated conditions. Uniqueness asks that any two objects satisfying them be equal. A contradiction proof may establish uniqueness by assuming two distinct candidates. It does not supply the existence argument.

Summary

  • A universal statement concerns every member of its specified domain. An existential statement requires at least one witness in that domain.
  • Proposition 1.1 and Equation (1.1) give negations of connectives, implications and quantified statements. Negating an implication retains its hypothesis and negates its conclusion.
  • An implication is equivalent to its contrapositive. Its converse needs a separate proof. An equivalence asserts both directions.
  • Proposition 1.3 and Theorem 1.4 illustrate direct proof, contraposition and contradiction. The number 2\sqrt22​ is irrational.

A limit definition will specify the order in which an accuracy and an index are chosen. The quantifier rules here allow us to state exactly when such a definition fails.
