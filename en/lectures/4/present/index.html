Order and completeness in sequence limits

An error bound can prove a proposed limit. Order and completeness also prove that a limit exists before its value is known. We use a supremum to prove monotone convergence, construct the number eee, and prove that every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence.

Uses.

  • Summary 2: A supremum is a least upper bound. Every nonempty real set bounded above has one (R). Bernoulli gives (1+h)n≥1+nh(1+h)^n\geq 1+nh(1+h)n≥1+nh for h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1. The Archimedean property supplies integer cutoffs. Standing fact E supplies the school trigonometric bounds and positive-base powers.

  • Summary 3: Convergence means an error below each positive tolerance eventually. Limits are unique; sums, products and quotients with nonzero denominator limit obey the limit laws. For real ∣q∣<1|q|<1∣q∣<1, qn→0q^n\to 0qn→0.

4.3 Monotone convergence

A real sequence is nondecreasing if an+1≥ana_{n+1}\geq a_nan+1​≥an​ for every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N. It is nonincreasing if all these inequalities are reversed. A sequence with either property is called monotone. These definitions permit equality of consecutive terms.

Theorem 4.5 (Monotone convergence). A nondecreasing real sequence that is bounded above converges to the supremum of its set of terms. A nonincreasing real sequence that is bounded below converges to the infimum of its set of terms.

Proof. Suppose (an)(a_n)(an​) is nondecreasing and bounded above. The set A={an:n∈N}A=\{a_n:n\in\N\}A={an​:n∈N} is nonempty and bounded above. By R, let s=sup⁡As=\sup As=supA. Given ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, the number s−εs-\varepsilons−ε is not an upper bound. Thus some aNa_NaN​ satisfies s−ε<aN≤ss-\varepsilon<a_N\leq ss−ε<aN​≤s. For every n≥Nn\geq Nn≥N, monotonicity gives s−ε<aN≤an≤s.s-\varepsilon<a_N\leq a_n\leq s.s−ε<aN​≤an​≤s. Hence ∣an−s∣<ε|a_n-s|<\varepsilon∣an​−s∣<ε, proving an→sa_n\to san​→s.

If (an)(a_n)(an​) is nonincreasing and bounded below, then (−an)(-a_n)(−an​) is nondecreasing and bounded above. The proved case supplies its limit. The scalar-multiple law gives a limit for (an)(a_n)(an​). Negating the least upper bound of {−an}\{-a_n\}{−an​} gives the greatest lower bound of {an}\{a_n\}{an​}, as asserted. 

The bound is essential. The sequence an=na_n=nan​=n is nondecreasing and has no real limit. More generally, if a nondecreasing sequence is unbounded above, then it tends to +∞+\infty+∞. Given M∈RM\in\RM∈R, choose a term aN>Ma_N>MaN​>M. Every later term is at least aNa_NaN​, so it also exceeds MMM.

A proof using monotone convergence has two separate obligations: establish the order of successive terms and supply a bound. Solving an equation for a possible limit does not establish either obligation. Sheet 4 applies this distinction to recursive sequences.

4.5 Subsequences and Bolzano–Weierstrass

A subsequence of (an)(a_n)(an​) is a sequence (ank)(a_{n_k})(ank​​) whose indices n1<n2<⋯n_1<n_2<\cdotsn1​<n2​<⋯ are strictly increasing natural numbers. Induction gives nk≥kn_k\geq knk​≥k for every k∈Nk\in\Nk∈N. If an→aa_n\to aan​→a, then every subsequence converges to aaa: an original cutoff NNN works whenever k≥Nk\geq Nk≥N, since then nk≥k≥Nn_k\geq k\geq Nnk​≥k≥N.

For example, the even-index subsequence of (−1)n(-1)^n(−1)n is constantly 1. The odd-index subsequence is constantly −1-1−1. Both subsequences converge, although the original sequence does not. Having one convergent subsequence does not force the full sequence to converge.

Theorem 4.7 (Bolzano–Weierstrass). Every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence. If all its terms lie in a closed interval [a,b][a,b][a,b], the subsequence’s limit also belongs to [a,b][a,b][a,b].

The proof repeatedly halves an interval and keeps a half containing infinitely many indices of the sequence. Figure 4.1 shows one possible chain of such intervals.

Proof. Let (xn)(x_n)(xn​) be the bounded sequence. Choose a closed interval I0=[a0,b0]I_0=[a_0,b_0]I0​=[a0​,b0​] of positive length containing all terms.
