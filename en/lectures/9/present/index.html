Lecture 9

Implicit derivatives and mean value theorems

A relation may specify a differentiable branch without solving explicitly for it. A parameter may specify both coordinates of a curve. We compute derivatives in these forms, then connect local derivatives to changes across an interval. The mean value theorem gives that connection without assuming continuity of the derivative.

Uses.

  • Summary 5: Continuity is relative to the domain. Order passes to finite limits, including one-sided limits.

  • Summary 6: The intermediate value theorem supplies roots between opposite endpoint signs. The constructed constant eee satisfies 2<e<32<e<32<e<3.

  • Summary 7: Continuous functions on closed bounded intervals attain their extreme values. A derivative is a finite quotient limit and implies continuity.

  • Summary 8: Arithmetic, chain and inverse rules apply with their stated domains. Elementary derivative formulas are proved, and a derivative need not be continuous.

9.3 Interior extrema and Rolle’s theorem

A function f:A→Rf:A\to\Rf:A→R has a local maximum at c∈Ac\in Ac∈A if some δ>0\delta>0δ>0 gives f(x)≤f(c)f(x)\leq f(c)f(x)≤f(c) for all x∈Ax\in Ax∈A with ∣x−c∣<δ|x-c|<\delta∣x−c∣<δ. A local minimum reverses the inequality. These are local extrema. Unlike a global extremum, the comparison is only with inputs in one neighbourhood.

Theorem 9.1 (Fermat’s theorem). If fff has a local extremum at an interior point ccc of its interval domain and is differentiable at ccc, then f′(c)=0f'(c)=0f′(c)=0.

Proof. At a local maximum, sufficiently small positive increments satisfy [f(c+h)−f(c)]/h≤0[f(c+h)-f(c)]/h\leq0[f(c+h)−f(c)]/h≤0. The same numerator for negative increments gives a quotient at least zero. Both one-sided quotient limits equal the finite derivative. Passing the inequalities to these limits gives f′(c)≤0f'(c)\leq0f′(c)≤0 and f′(c)≥0f'(c)\geq0f′(c)≥0, hence f′(c)=0f'(c)=0f′(c)=0. For a local minimum, apply this maximum argument to −f-f−f. Its derivative is −f′(c)-f'(c)−f′(c), so the conclusion is unchanged. 

The hypotheses distinguish three different cases. The function x2x^2x2 on [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1] has an interior minimum at zero and its derivative is zero there. The function ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ also has an interior minimum there, but has no derivative. The identity on [0,1][0,1][0,1] has its minimum at the endpoint zero, with right derivative 1. Fermat’s theorem does not assert a zero derivative in the last two cases. A zero derivative is not sufficient for an extremum: x3x^3x3 has derivative zero at zero and takes negative values on one side and positive values on the other.

Theorem 9.2 (Rolle’s theorem). Let a<ba<ba<b. If f:[a,b]→Rf:[a,b]\to\Rf:[a,b]→R is continuous, differentiable on (a,b)(a,b)(a,b), and f(a)=f(b)f(a)=f(b)f(a)=f(b), then some c∈(a,b)c\in(a,b)c∈(a,b) satisfies f′(c)=0f'(c)=0f′(c)=0.

Proof. If fff is constant, every interior point has derivative zero. Otherwise the extreme value theorem gives a maximum and a minimum, and at least one of these values differs from the common endpoint value. An input attaining that different value cannot be either endpoint. It is an interior global, hence local, extremum. Fermat’s theorem gives zero derivative there. 

For example, f(x)=x2−3xf(x)=x^2-3xf(x)=x2−3x on [0,3][0,3][0,3] has equal endpoint values zero. It is continuous and differentiable, so Rolle’s theorem applies. Its derivative is 2x−32x-32x−3, which vanishes at the interior point c=3/2c=3/2c=3/2. In contrast, ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ on [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1] has equal endpoint values but no point with derivative zero. Its failure of differentiability at zero prevents application of Rolle’s theorem.

9.5 Monotonicity, constancy and bounds

Recall that f:I→Rf:I\to\Rf:I→R is nondecreasing if f(u)≤f(v)f(u)\leq f(v)f(u)≤f(v) whenever u<vu<vu<v are in III. It is nonincreasing if the output inequality is reversed. These definitions permit equal outputs at distinct inputs.

Theorem 9.4 (Derivative signs on an interval). Let fff be continuous on a real interval III and differentiable at every interior point. If f′≥0f'\geq0f′≥0 throughout the interior, then fff is nondecreasing on III. If f′>0f'>0f′>0 there, then fff is strictly increasing. The corresponding statements with nonpositive or negative derivatives give nonincreasing or strictly decreasing functions. If f′=0f'=0f′=0 throughout the interior, then fff is constant on III.

Proof. Choose any u<vu<vu<v in III. The closed interval [u,v][u,v][u,v] is contained in III. If the derivative is zero throughout, the displayed difference is zero for every pair, proving constancy. On an interval with at most one point, the same conclusions hold directly. 

Conversely, a differentiable nondecreasing function has nonnegative derivative: for positive increments the numerator and denominator of the quotient are nonnegative, and for negative increments both are nonpositive. Every quotient is nonnegative, so its limit is nonnegative. Strict increase does not force a strictly positive derivative everywhere, as x3x^3x3 at zero demonstrates. The interval hypothesis for constancy also matters. A function equal to zero on (−∞,0)(-\infty,0)(−∞,0) and 1 on (0,∞)(0,\infty)(0,∞) has derivative zero at every point of its domain, but is not constant on that disconnected domain.

Summary

  • Implicit differentiation computes derivatives of a given differentiable branch. Solving for its derivative requires a nonzero coefficient. It does not establish existence of the branch.
  • Equations (9.2) and (9.3): an invertible coordinate parameter with x′≠0x'\ne0x′=0 gives graph derivatives y′/x′y'/x'y′/x′ and (y′′x′−y′x′′)/(x′)3(y''x'-y'x'')/(x')^3(y′′x′−y′x′′)/(x′)3. The first requires differentiable coordinates, and the second requires twice differentiable coordinates.
  • Theorems 9.1 and 9.2: an interior differentiable extremum has derivative zero. Equal endpoint values and Rolle’s other hypotheses guarantee such a zero derivative somewhere inside.
  • Theorem 9.3: continuity on [a,b][a,b][a,b] and differentiability on (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) give an interior derivative equal to the secant slope.
  • Theorem 9.4 and Equation (9.4): derivative signs control monotonicity on intervals. Zero derivatives imply constancy, and bounded derivatives give finite bounds on output changes.
