Lecture 2

Functions and the real numbers

Limits concern the values of functions and the order of real numbers. We specify the functions we use, prove the estimates needed for sequence limits and state completeness. Completeness will supply an integer beyond any prescribed real bound.

Uses.

  • Summary 1: Sets specify domains. An equivalence requires both implications. Quantifiers specify the order of choices. The number 2\sqrt 22​ is irrational.

The function does not attain a negative value even though its codomain contains such values.

2.2 Composition and inverses

2.3 Induction and Bernoulli’s inequality

Mathematical induction proves a property P(n)P(n)P(n) for every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N by establishing a starting case and an implication from one index to the next. The principle asserts that if P(1)P(1)P(1) holds and P(n)⇒P(n+1)P(n)\Rightarrow P(n+1)P(n)⇒P(n+1) for every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N, then P(n)P(n)P(n) holds for every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N. We use induction as a basic property of the natural numbers.

The induction hypothesis is a statement at an arbitrary fixed index nnn. It is an assumption used to prove the implication to n+1n+1n+1. It is not an assumption that the final conclusion already holds at every index. The starting case is needed to apply the successive implications.

Theorem 2.1 (Bernoulli’s inequality). For every h∈Rh\in\Rh∈R with h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1 and every n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N, (1+h)n≥1+nh.(1+h)^n\geq1+nh.(1+h)n≥1+nh.

Proof. Fix h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1. We argue by induction on nnn. At n=1n=1n=1 the assertion is equality. Suppose it holds at nnn. Since 1+h≥01+h\geq01+h≥0, multiplication by 1+h1+h1+h preserves the inequality. Expansion gives (1+h)n+1≥(1+nh)(1+h)=1+(n+1)h+nh2≥1+(n+1)h.(1+h)^{n+1}\geq(1+nh)(1+h) =1+(n+1)h+nh^2\geq1+(n+1)h.(1+h)n+1≥(1+nh)(1+h)=1+(n+1)h+nh2≥1+(n+1)h. The last inequality follows from n≥1n\geq1n≥1 and h2≥0h^2\geq0h2≥0. This proves the induction step and hence the theorem. 

The restriction h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1 makes the multiplication in the proof valid. The factor 1+nh1+nh1+nh need not be positive. The proof does not multiply an inequality by that factor, so its sign does not affect the argument. The endpoint h=−1h=-1h=−1 is included, since its multiplier is zero.

2.4 Absolute value and error estimates

For r≥0r\geq0r≥0, the relation ∣x∣≤r|x|\leq r∣x∣≤r is equivalent to −r≤x≤r-r\leq x\leq r−r≤x≤r. This converts absolute-value bounds into ordered inequalities. Proof. The first inequality was proved by the ordered bounds above. For the second, apply the first to x=(x−y)+yx=(x-y)+yx=(x−y)+y to obtain ∣x∣−∣y∣≤∣x−y∣|x|-|y|\leq|x-y|∣x∣−∣y∣≤∣x−y∣. For a proposed value a∈Ra\in\Ra∈R, the number ∣x−a∣|x-a|∣x−a∣ measures the error in using xxx for aaa. The triangle inequality then bounds a total error by the sum of two intermediate errors: ∣x−a∣≤∣x−b∣+∣b−a∣(x,a,b∈R).|x-a|\leq|x-b|+|b-a|\qquad(x,a,b\in\R).∣x−a∣≤∣x−b∣+∣b−a∣(x,a,b∈R). This estimate does not depend on the order of the three points on the real line. It is useful precisely when that order is not known.

2.5 Bounds and completeness

Let A⊆RA\subseteq\RA⊆R be nonempty. A real number uuu is an upper bound of AAA if x≤ux\leq ux≤u for every x∈Ax\in Ax∈A. The set is bounded above if an upper bound exists. A lower bound reverses this inequality. The set is bounded if it has both an upper and a lower bound. A maximum of AAA is an upper bound that belongs to AAA.

Equivalently, AAA is bounded if some M>0M>0M>0 satisfies ∣x∣≤M|x|\leq M∣x∣≤M for all x∈Ax\in Ax∈A. To verify this equivalence, suppose ℓ\ellℓ and uuu are lower and upper bounds. Conversely, such an MMM supplies the lower bound −M-M−M and the upper bound MMM.

A supremum, written sup⁡A\sup AsupA, is a least upper bound. Its defining conditions, for s∈Rs\in\Rs∈R, are x≤sfor every x∈A,s≤ufor every upper bound u of A.x\leq s\quad\text{for every }x\in A, \qquad s\leq u\quad\text{for every upper bound }u\text{ of }A.x≤sfor every x∈A,s≤ufor every upper bound u of A. Two least upper bounds are each at most the other, so they are equal. Thus the notation sup⁡A\sup AsupA denotes a unique number when a least upper bound exists.

The supremum need not belong to AAA. Dually, the infimum inf⁡A\inf AinfA is a greatest lower bound.

For example, the interval A=(0,1)A=(0,1)A=(0,1) has supremum 1 and no maximum. Every member is less than 1, so 1 is an upper bound. To prove it is least, let u<1u<1u<1. If u≤0u\leq0u≤0, the member 1/21/21/2 exceeds uuu. If 0<u<10<u<10<u<1, the member (u+1)/2(u+1)/2(u+1)/2 exceeds uuu and is less than 1. Thus no u<1u<1u<1 is an upper bound. More generally, if s=sup⁡As=\sup As=supA, then for each ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 there exists x∈Ax\in Ax∈A with s−ε<x≤ss-\varepsilon<x\leq ss−ε<x≤s. Otherwise s−εs-\varepsilons−ε would be an upper bound smaller than sss. The element xxx may depend on ε\varepsilonε. This consequence of leastness will construct limits of monotone sequences.

Standing fact R.

The real numbers have the following completeness property: every nonempty subset of R\RR that is bounded above has a supremum in R\RR. We assume this property and do not construct the real numbers here. The ordinary field and order rules of school arithmetic remain in force.

Completeness does not say that every set has a largest element. The open interval just considered has a supremum without a largest element. It also does not say that an unbounded set has a finite supremum. Both nonemptiness and an upper bound are hypotheses of standing fact R.

Density locates the failure of completeness in the rational numbers. Consider the set S={q∈Q:0<q<2}S=\{q\in\Q:0<q<\sqrt2\}S={q∈Q:0<q<2​}. Summary

  • A function includes its domain and codomain. Its image consists of attained values. An inverse exists exactly for a bijection.
  • Theorem 2.1: (1+h)n≥1+nh(1+h)^n\geq1+nh(1+h)n≥1+nh for h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1 and n∈Nn\in\Nn∈N. The proof is by induction.
  • Theorem 2.2: the triangle and reverse triangle inequalities bound sums and changes of absolute values.
  • Standing fact R gives a supremum to every nonempty real set bounded above. A supremum is a least upper bound, not necessarily a maximum.
  • Theorem 2.3 supplies integer cutoffs. Consequently, Q\QQ is dense in R\RR but does not have the completeness property.

Lecture 3 uses the estimates and integer choices to prove sequence limits. The least-upper-bound property itself will establish convergence of monotone bounded sequences in Lecture 4.
