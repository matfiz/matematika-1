Lecture 6

Standard limits and the intermediate value theorem

Standard limits describe elementary functions close to zero. We prove the logarithmic and exponential limits from the construction of eee, before using any derivative. We then prove that a continuous real function on an interval takes every value between two of its values. This gives a method for locating an unknown root with a certified error.

Uses.

  • Summary 1: The number 2\sqrt 22​ is irrational.

  • Summary 2: The Archimedean property supplies integer cutoffs. Bernoulli gives (1+h)n≥1+nh(1+h)^n\geq 1+nh(1+h)n≥1+nh for h≥−1h\geq-1h≥−1.

  • Summary 3: Sequence limits obey arithmetic laws. Geometric powers satisfy qn→0q^n\to 0qn→0 for ∣q∣<1|q|<1∣q∣<1.

  • Summary 4: Bounded monotone sequences converge. The sequences un=(1+1/n)nu_n=(1+1/n)^nun​=(1+1/n)n and vn=(1+1/n)n+1v_n=(1+1/n)^{n+1}vn​=(1+1/n)n+1 satisfy un≤e≤vnu_n\leq e\leq v_nun​≤e≤vn​ and converge to eee, with 2<e<32<e<32<e<3.

  • Summary 5: Function limits obey arithmetic, squeeze and qualified composition laws. Continuity preserves sequence limits. In radians, sin⁡x/x→1\sin x/x\to 1sinx/x→1 as x→0x\to 0x→0.

  • Standing fact E: School logarithm, exponential and positive-power laws, monotonicity and continuity are assumed. Angles are in radians.

6.3 Asymptotic equivalence

Definition 6.3 (Asymptotic equivalence). For real functions fff and ggg on a common domain with cluster point ccc, write f(x)∼g(x)f(x)\sim g(x)f(x)∼g(x) as x→cx\to cx→c if ggg is nonzero throughout some deleted neighbourhood of ccc and f(x)/g(x)→1f(x)/g(x)\to1f(x)/g(x)→1 there.

In particular, the standard limits say ln⁡(1+x)∼x,ex−1∼x,sin⁡x∼x,1−cos⁡x∼x2/2(x→0).\ln(1+x)\sim x,\qquad e^x-1\sim x,\qquad \sin x\sim x,\qquad 1-\cos x\sim x^2/2 \qquad(x\to0).ln(1+x)∼x,ex−1∼x,sinx∼x,1−cosx∼x2/2(x→0). The statement f∼gf\sim gf∼g alone does not require that either function tend to zero. Constants also qualify.

Equivalents may be multiplied and divided. More precisely, suppose f∼gf\sim gf∼g and h∼kh\sim kh∼k on the same approach domain. 6.5 Intermediate values and bisection

Theorem 6.4 (Intermediate value theorem). Let a<ba<ba<b and let f:[a,b]→Rf:[a,b]\to\Rf:[a,b]→R be continuous. If y∈Ry\in\Ry∈R lies between f(a)f(a)f(a) and f(b)f(b)f(b), including either endpoint value, then there is c∈[a,b]c\in[a,b]c∈[a,b] with f(c)=yf(c)=yf(c)=y.

Start with a0=aa_0=aa0​=a and b0=bb_0=bb0​=b. At stage nnn, take the midpoint mn=(an+bn)/2m_n=(a_n+b_n)/2mn​=(an​+bn​)/2. If f(mn)=yf(m_n)=yf(mn​)=y, the conclusion is proved. If f(mn)<yf(m_n)<yf(mn​)<y, retain [mn,bn][m_n,b_n][mn​,bn​]. Otherwise retain [an,mn][a_n,m_n][an​,mn​]. If no stage gives equality, this constructs nested closed intervals with f(an)<y<f(bn),bn−an=b−a2n.f(a_n)<y<f(b_n),\qquad b_n-a_n=\frac{b-a}{2^n}.f(an​)<y<f(bn​),bn​−an​=2nb−a​. The lengths tend to zero, so bn→cb_n\to cbn​→c. Continuity gives f(an)→f(c)f(a_n)\to f(c)f(an​)→f(c) and f(bn)→f(c)f(b_n)\to f(c)f(bn​)→f(c). Passing both inequalities to the limit gives f(c)≤y≤f(c)f(c)\leq y\leq f(c)f(c)≤y≤f(c). Thus f(c)=yf(c)=yf(c)=y, as required. 

The domain condition matters. The function r(x)=x2−2r(x)=x^2-2r(x)=x2−2 restricted to Q∩[1,2]\Q\cap[1,2]Q∩[1,2] is continuous relative to that domain. Its endpoint values are −1-1−1 and 2. It has no zero in the domain, since a rational square root of 2 would contradict Lecture 1. This domain is not a real interval.

The theorem also shows that a continuous image of a real interval is an interval. Here an interval means a subset containing every real number between any two of its members. Given two image values, choose domain points producing them and restrict fff to the closed interval between those points. The theorem supplies every intermediate image value. The original interval may be open or unbounded. The chosen closed subinterval still lies in it.

The bisection construction also gives an error bound for an unknown root. After kkk halvings, a retained interval has length (b−a)/2k(b-a)/2^k(b−a)/2k. If it contains the root ccc, its midpoint zkz_kzk​ satisfies ∣zk−c∣≤b−a2k+1.\begin{equation} \label{eq:6:bisection-error} |z_k-c|\leq\frac{b-a}{2^{k+1}}. \end{equation}∣zk​−c∣≤2k+1b−a​.(6.3) Both distances from the midpoint to its endpoints equal the right-hand side. Every point of the retained interval is within this half-length of its midpoint. Applying this to the root gives the displayed error bound.

Summary

  • Theorems 6.1 and 6.2: the standard logarithmic and exponential quotients tend to 1, and the real-power quotient tends to its exponent α\alphaα. The power uses a positive base.
  • Equation (6.2): tan⁡x/x→1\tan x/x\to1tanx/x→1 and (1−cos⁡x)/x2→1/2(1-\cos x)/x^2\to1/2(1−cosx)/x2→1/2 in radians as x→0x\to0x→0.
  • Asymptotic equivalence means a quotient tending to 1 with a nonzero comparison function near the approach point. It permits multiplication and division, but does not justify cancellation in a difference.
  • For positive bases, ab=exp⁡(bln⁡a)a^b=\exp(b\ln a)ab=exp(blna) reduces a power limit to an exponent limit. The patterns 000^000, ∞0\infty^0∞0 and 1∞1^\infty1∞ are indeterminate.
  • Theorem 6.4 and Equation (6.3): a continuous function on a closed real interval takes every intermediate endpoint value. Bisection retains a root with an explicit midpoint error bound.
