Lecture 6

Standard limits and the intermediate value theorem

What this lecture does

Standard limits describe elementary functions close to zero. We prove the logarithmic and exponential limits from the construction of eee, before using any derivative. We then prove that a continuous real function on an interval takes every value between two of its values. This gives a method for locating an unknown root with a certified error.

6.3 Asymptotic equivalence

Definition 6.3 (Asymptotic equivalence). For real functions fff and ggg on a common domain with cluster point ccc, write f(x)∼g(x)f(x)\sim g(x)f(x)∼g(x) as x→cx\to cx→c if ggg is nonzero throughout some deleted neighbourhood of ccc and f(x)/g(x)→1f(x)/g(x)\to1f(x)/g(x)→1 there.

6.5 Intermediate values and bisection

Theorem 6.4 (Intermediate value theorem). Let a<ba<ba<b and let f:[a,b]→Rf:[a,b]\to\Rf:[a,b]→R be continuous. If y∈Ry\in\Ry∈R lies between f(a)f(a)f(a) and f(b)f(b)f(b), including either endpoint value, then there is c∈[a,b]c\in[a,b]c∈[a,b] with f(c)=yf(c)=yf(c)=y.

If no stage gives equality, this constructs nested closed intervals with f(an)<y<f(bn),bn−an=b−a2n.f(a_n)<y<f(b_n),\qquad b_n-a_n=\frac{b-a}{2^n}.f(an​)<y<f(bn​),bn​−an​=2nb−a​. The domain condition matters. The function r(x)=x2−2r(x)=x^2-2r(x)=x2−2 restricted to Q∩[1,2]\Q\cap[1,2]Q∩[1,2] is continuous relative to that domain. Its endpoint values are −1-1−1 and 2. It has no zero in the domain, since a rational square root of 2 would contradict Lecture 1. This domain is not a real interval.

The theorem also shows that a continuous image of a real interval is an interval. Here an interval means a subset containing every real number between any two of its members. Given two image values, choose domain points producing them and restrict fff to the closed interval between those points. The theorem supplies every intermediate image value. The original interval may be open or unbounded. The chosen closed subinterval still lies in it.

The bisection construction also gives an error bound for an unknown root. After kkk halvings, a retained interval has length (b−a)/2k(b-a)/2^k(b−a)/2k. Every point of the retained interval is within this half-length of its midpoint. Applying this to the root gives the displayed error bound.

Summary

  • Theorems 6.1 and 6.2: the standard logarithmic and exponential quotients tend to 1, and the real-power quotient tends to its exponent α\alphaα. The power uses a positive base.
  • Equation (6.2): tan⁡x/x→1\tan x/x\to1tanx/x→1 and (1−cos⁡x)/x2→1/2(1-\cos x)/x^2\to1/2(1−cosx)/x2→1/2 in radians as x→0x\to0x→0.
  • Asymptotic equivalence means a quotient tending to 1 with a nonzero comparison function near the approach point. It permits multiplication and division, but does not justify cancellation in a difference.
  • For positive bases, ab=exp⁡(bln⁡a)a^b=\exp(b\ln a)ab=exp(blna) reduces a power limit to an exponent limit. The patterns 000^000, ∞0\infty^0∞0 and 1∞1^\infty1∞ are indeterminate.
  • Theorem 6.4 and Equation (6.3): a continuous function on a closed real interval takes every intermediate endpoint value. Bisection retains a root with an explicit midpoint error bound.
